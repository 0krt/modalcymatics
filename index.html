Plate

edge condition
dispersion
a real plate is fourth-order (biharmonic), so doubling the frequency scales the wavenumber by √2, not 2. the membrane setting is the ordinary wave equation for comparison.

free — rim moves freely, the classic loose Chladni plate.
supported — rim pinned to zero but free to tilt.
clamped — rim pinned and held flat (w = w′ = 0). the stiffest case, so every mode sits higher, and a boundary layer forms at the rim.
mixed c–f — clamped along x, free along y: a rectangular plate held on two opposite edges only.

the rectangle uses beam eigenfunctions per axis (the Ritz construction); the disc uses Bessel modes, with the clamped case needing the modified-Bessel companion. the ring is supported on both rims, and the equilateral triangle uses the exact Lamé modes built by reflecting six plane waves — both are only defined for the supported case here.

on-screen size shrinks the sheet, and it is a real size change, not a zoom: the drive frequency fixes the wavelength, so a smaller plate fits fewer of them. every mode climbs as 1/s² (1/s for a membrane), and at a fixed frequency the figure coarsens — half the size shows the pattern the full plate made at a quarter the frequency.

the sheet's real dimensions — size, thickness and material — live under Real units; the size there is the full-frame plate, so the drive's hertz stay put as you shrink it.

Real units

converts the abstract frequency scale into hertz for an actual sheet: f = π F h cp / (2 L² √12), with cp = √(E / ρ(1−ν²)). the fourth-power in L is the whole story of plate tuning — double the size and every pitch drops two octaves. thickness scales it linearly the other way.

these controls are live only while real hz is armed under Audio, since that is what plays the sheet you have drawn. the hertz figures beside the drive and in the mode list are read off this calibration either way.

Excitation point

a driver sitting on a mode's nodal line cannot excite it at all. drag on the plate to move the driver and watch half the catalogue vanish. corner drive couples to everything.

shift-click the plate to pin a damping finger (up to four; shift-click one again to lift it). a finger loads the plate hard: it eats the mode's q and weakens the drive's grip on it at once, so at the top of the finger damping range a mode with an antinode under your fingertip is silenced outright rather than merely blurred. a mode with a node there rings on untouched, which is exactly how you pick a single figure out of a degenerate pair by hand.

Modes

the modes the current drive is actually exciting, strongest first.

rectangle — (m,n): the number of half-wave divisions along each axis. (3,2) has three across and two down. natural frequency goes as m² + n² for a square plate; stretching the aspect ratio or clamping the edges pushes it up.

equilateral triangle — (p,q): the two lattice indices of the Lamé solution. frequency goes as p² + pq + q². the ′ twin is the mirror-antisymmetric partner — it exists only when p ≠ q, which is exactly why the diagonal modes are single and every other level comes in a degenerate pair.

disc / ring — (n,s): n nodal diameters (lines through the centre), s nodal circles (rings). a trailing ′ marks the rotated twin of the same mode — it has the same frequency but sits 90°/n around, and the drive point decides how the two mix.

f is the mode's natural frequency in the same units as the frequency slider — park the slider on one of these numbers and that mode rings on its own. equal f values are degenerate: they resonate together and superpose, which is where the intricate figures come from. the bar is how hard the drive is currently pushing each one.

Presets

patch file
export json writes the whole patch to a file — plate, drive, grains, colours, rotation, both lfos and their targets, and the midi cc map. import json reads one back and applies it wholesale, so a patch travels between machines and browsers.

the built-in preset list above is unchanged: it only names a handful of fields and leaves everything else where you left it. an imported json replaces the lot.

Drive

the plate is a linear filter: waveform only matters as harmonic content. each partial excites whatever modes it lands near. relative partial phase cannot move the sand — only the amplitude spectrum can.

partial focus is a bandpass across the harmonic series. at off the whole spectrum drives the plate; sweep it up and it isolates one partial at a time, so you can hear and see a single harmonic pull its own set of modes. focus width is how many neighbouring partials come with it — narrow it to about 0.2 for a clean solo.

controls grey out when the waveform does not use them: a sine already has one partial.

snap to resonance quantises the drive to the nearest eigenfrequency of the current plate, so a sweep lands on figures instead of passing between them. the slider still moves freely — only the value it reports is snapped. the scroll wheel then steps mode by mode.

Audio

synthesises the actual drive signal — the same partial amplitudes and phases that are feeding the plate, as a periodic wave. changing waveform, partial count, tilt or pulse width changes what you hear and what the plate does, together.

pitch per unit sets the audible hertz for one unit of modal frequency, since the frequency slider is in plate units, not hertz. at 8 Hz/unit a drive of f 25 sounds at 200 Hz.

real hz ignores that slider and plays the frequency the plate would actually sound at, from the size, thickness and material set under Real units below — you hear the sheet you drew, and those controls come alive.

LFO

two free-running modulators. each has a list of targets, and each target its own bipolar amount — the fraction of that parameter's full range the lfo sweeps. modulation is added on top of the slider rather than replacing it, so the slider stays yours: move it and the whole modulated swing moves with it. the readout turns blue while a value is being driven.

plate shape, edge condition and dispersion are steppable targets — the lfo quantises to a whole setting and only fires on a change, so a slow square wave flips between two plates instead of thrashing the solver.

s&h holds a fresh random value for a whole cycle, so rate is the step rate. phase offsets one lfo against the other. rate and depth are themselves midi targets. settings persist locally.

Style

grains is the sand simulation. the other two draw the same time-averaged field directly, per pixel, with no particles — the figure the sand would settle into, at any resolution.

smooth has three readings of it: nodal lines traces the still curves themselves, density is the settled sand as a continuous glow (softness is how far it spreads off the node), contours draws the amplitude as a topographic map — the nodes bold, the antinodes as nested rings.

liquid pours a glossy liquid into the nodal channels: fill is how much there is, depth how tall the beads stand, light angle where the key light sits. chrome is a mirror, gloss a tinted resin in the sand colour, water is clear and refracts the ruled plate beneath it.

morph time makes every change settle the way a real plate does: each mode rings up or down with its own time constant, Q over π times its frequency, so low and sharp modes move slowly and high or damped ones quickly. the slider scales all of them; at zero changes are instant. changing the shape, edge or dispersion swaps the whole set of modes, so those cross-fade over the same time instead. in a momentary mode, letting go lets the figure ring down.

Grains

momentary · hold the plate
coarse sand collects at nodes. very fine powder does the opposite — acoustic streaming in the air carries it to the antinodes. which of the two is on the plate is picked under Rendering → medium; both at once runs two populations in two colours, so the nodal and antinodal figures appear together, and fine fraction is the split.

feed rate continuously recycles grains back onto the plate, oldest first, as if you were sprinkling steadily while it runs. at zero the population is static.

even feed gives each population its own recycle cursor. it is what stops both at once from pulsing: with one cursor for the whole population the block of freshly scattered grains sweeps through the sand first and the powder after it, so each figure blurs and sharpens in turn, once per turnover — a beat at the feed rate, most obvious with feed high. switch it off to get that sweep back.

momentary makes the plate an instrument you hold: it only sounds, and the grains only move, while the pointer is down on it (or a midi note is held). fade shows nothing at rest — let go and the plate stops being fed and its grains are taken off, oldest first, at feed rate plus kill rate of the population per second, so a sum of 4 clears it in a quarter of a second. press again and they land as a fresh sprinkle. noise keeps the grains, but on release sprinkles them back, at the same rate, onto a maximally even spread — as if a burst of noise had shaken the figure out — ready for the next press to draw it again. m steps through the three.

hop threshold is the local amplitude a grain needs before it will move at all — real sand only migrates once the plate's acceleration beats gravity. jitter is random bouncing, scaled by the local amplitude, so it competes with the crawl and fuzzes the lines.

motion is the live plate oscillation — the real drive is far too fast to see, so this slows it down. it does not affect where the grains settle.

medium picks what is on the plate: coarse sand crawling to the nodes, fine powder blown to the antinodes, or both populations at once in two colours. the rest of their behaviour — count, feed, crawl, the fine fraction — lives under Grains.

field: motion shows instantaneous displacement, rms shows the time-averaged amplitude the grains actually respond to.

trails paints the previous frame back over the new one, so a grain leaves a fading streak behind it instead of jumping. the number is the decay per frame: at 90% a grain from ten frames ago is still a third as bright, at 30% it is gone almost at once. it costs nothing in the physics — the sand settles exactly where it always did, you are just seeing the path it took. it reads best with feed rate up, and turns a spinning plate into long arcs.

plate spin turns the whole plate — sheet, field and grains together — at a steady rate, and plate angle is a fixed offset on top of it. it is a camera move, not a physical one: the modes and the sand behave exactly as they would on a plate standing still. a square or triangular plate shrinks a little once rotation is armed, so its corners never clip the frame while it turns.

canvas is the ground behind the plate, and it fills the letterbox surround too, so a framed export lands on one flat colour. the grain colours are ordinary parameters — hue, saturation and lightness — so a controller or an lfo can drive them. the fine hue only shows in both at once. the driver dot marks the excitation point — switch it off for a clean capture.

add a mapping, pick a target, hit learn and move a knob. the monitor above shows every incoming message, so you can confirm the controller is reaching the page at all.

quicker: every mappable control in the panel has an M button beside it. click it and move a knob — it then shows the cc it is bound to. click again to re-learn, right-click (or shift-click) to unmap.

the cc box takes a number by hand if you would rather read it off the controller's manual than coax it into sending the right knob — type it, or leave it empty to unbind.

notes → resonances plays the plate from a keyboard. the lowest note lands on the plate's lowest eigenfrequency and every semitone above it steps one rung up the ladder, so a chromatic run walks the whole catalogue of figures in order. it is not a pitch — the rungs are however far apart the plate's own resonances happen to be, which is the point. notes past the top of the ladder hold at the highest one, and changing the plate re-ranks everything under your fingers.

lo / hi set the range the full 0–127 sweep covers — invert them to reverse the knob. smoothing interpolates between 7-bit steps so sweeps do not stair-step.

a mapping only holds a parameter while a cc is actually moving it. once the sweep settles it lets go, so a bound-but-silent knob never locks the slider, the arrow keys or an lfo out of that parameter. mappings persist locally.

About read me

What is this?
A Chladni plate is a flat sheet of metal with sand on it. Vibrate it at the right pitch and the sand stops being a mess and snaps into a sharp geometric pattern. Ernst Chladni was doing this in 1787; Napoleon watched it and put up prize money.

Why does the sand move?
A vibrating plate does not go up and down all in one piece. Parts of it swing hard while other parts barely move at all. The still lines are called nodes. Sand sitting on a violently shaking part gets thrown around until it happens to land somewhere still — and then it stays. So the sand is not drawing the vibration. It is drawing the places where there is no vibration. Fine powder does the opposite: it is light enough for the moving air to carry, so it piles up at the loudest spots instead.

What is a mode?
A plate cannot vibrate in just any shape. Like a guitar string that will only give you its fundamental and its overtones, a plate has a fixed catalogue of standing patterns it is willing to hold — its modes. Each one has its own pitch. Play that pitch and that pattern appears; play something in between and you mostly get mud. That is why sweeping a tone generator across a plate is so dramatic: nothing, nothing, nothing — then suddenly a perfect star.

What decides the pitches?
Size — bigger plates are lower, and steeply so: double the width and every pitch drops by a factor of four.
Stiffness and thickness — a thicker or stiffer plate is higher. Steel sings higher than aluminium of the same size.
Shape and how it is held — a square, a circle and a triangle have completely different catalogues. Clamping the rim rather than letting it flap raises every pitch and changes every pattern.

What makes the patterns intricate?
Sometimes two entirely different modes happen to share the same pitch — degenerate modes. They both wake up at once and add together, and where you touch the plate to drive it decides how they blend, so one frequency can give a whole family of figures. A driver sitting exactly on a mode's node cannot wake it at all — which is why moving the drive point makes half the catalogue vanish.
